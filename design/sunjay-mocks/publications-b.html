Wearing a cloak of Garuda

A practice note on borrowing a great bird's posture before you have earned its sky, and on what happens in the body when you do.

By Jason Ford / 14 Nov 2026 / 4 min read

Details Subscribe Back to contents

The first thing I notice is the weight. Before any wings, before any sky, the cloak is a matter of shoulders. I stand in the rehearsal room with my feet apart and let the imagined feathers settle across my back, and for a while nothing happens except that I become aware of how I usually carry myself, which is slightly apologetically.

Putting it on

Garuda, the great bird of the Himalayan traditions, is said to be born fully grown. That detail is what I keep returning to. We tend to approach practice as a long ladder, small first and large later. The bird refuses the ladder. It arrives complete, wings already open, and the work is not to grow but to stop shrinking.

In Bon and Buddhist iconography the Garuda (khyung) is hatched fully grown, which is why it stands for what is complete from the start.

So in the circle we begin by taking up space on purpose. Arms wide, chest open, gaze past the far wall. It looks foolish for about ninety seconds. Then it stops looking like anything, because everyone is too busy feeling it.

“The cloak does not make you larger. It shows you how much of your size you had been folding away.”

Nobody is asked to believe in the bird. We are asked to hold the posture and report honestly on what it does to attention. In my own work the first report is almost always the same: the breath drops lower, the jaw lets go, and the mind, which had been narrating, goes quiet to see what the body will do.

What the cloak asks

A costume that asks nothing is only a costume. This one asks for stillness at the top of the movement, the moment when the wings are fully spread and there is no further gesture to make. That is the hard part. Grotowski spoke of a via negativa, a way of removing the blocks rather than adding skills. The cloak works the same way: the bird is already there, and the practice is the subtraction of everything that says otherwise.

Jerzy Grotowski, Towards a Poor Theatre (1968), where “via negativa” names the removal of an actor's blocks.

Alone, this can turn grandiose. That is why we do it in company. Someone across the circle sees the moment you start to perform the bird instead of inhabiting it, and a glance is enough to bring you back. The laboratory model of paratheatre, in which a session is an investigation and not a show, is what keeps the cloak honest.

Antero Alli's paratheatre labs, which Para Theater counts among its lineages.

Taking it off

Taking it off is half of the practice, and the half most often skipped. We let the arms fall slowly, one vertebra at a time, and we name one thing we are carrying back into the room: a posture, a sentence, a decision. Then we sit. The cloak goes back on its peg, which is to say it goes back to being available rather than worn.

I come away from these sessions neither lighter nor heavier, but better fitted. The ordinary self turns out to be the cloak's lining, and I am a little more careful with it afterwards.
